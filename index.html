In euro, on the same contributions on the same days, at the issuers' net asset values or the published rate. The comparators carry no costs, and the portfolio's costs are in the Costs part. Returns since the first purchase are cumulative and not annualised. Differences are the comparator's return minus the portfolio's, in percentage points. The Mag7 fund's series starts on 4 November 2026, so its figures run from that day's close, against the portfolio's over the same days.

The band is the one chance alone produces over the same span: plus or minus two tracking errors times the square root of the years, where the tracking error is the standard deviation of the daily differences between the two series times the square root of 252.

A daily comparator's volatility, correlation, difference and band are shown from its 20th daily return, about a month, and its effects with them. The benchmark has values at month ends only, because the bond index has no public daily series. Its return runs from the first month end and is shown from the second, and its volatility, worst fall, correlation, difference and band come from monthly values times the square root of 12 and are shown from the sixth month end, since a tracking error from fewer monthly differences is not an estimate.

Volatility, worst falls and correlations become informative within months, and differences of return over years. Over the record's 22 trading days a difference says nothing about either rule set.

Where each difference came from

Figure 11 | Where each difference came from: from the portfolio's money-weighted return to the comparator's

As of 6 November 2026

Each difference is split into effects computed day by day, summed by month and then over the months. Implementation is reference portfolio A's return minus the portfolio's. Drift is reference portfolio B's return minus reference portfolio A's. Split is the difference in the equity share of the invested part times the equity fund's return in US dollars minus the bond fund's return, and the dollar is the same difference times the gap between the equity fund's return in euro and in US dollars, at the ECB's reference rates, so the two sum to the split in euro. The fund's other currencies stay inside its dollar return. Cash is the difference in cash weight times the return of the invested part.

For a comparator that holds other funds, the difference runs through the two funds held at the comparator's equity share without cash, which gives cash, split and the dollar by the same definitions. Regions is the comparator's equity share times the sum over the four regions of its weight in the region minus the held equity fund's, times the region's MSCI index return in euro, Brinson and Fachler's allocation term, with each fund's weights scaled to the four regions. A US dollar fund's own currency gap against the held fund's goes to the dollar, and within each region is the rest: the funds' choice of companies inside each region, their costs and their timing against the indices. The funds and the indices are valued at different times of the day, so a fund's gap against the indices can reach some tenths of a point on a day and reverse the next. The gap cancels between a comparator and the held fund when both are converted at the ECB's rate, and a gap under a tenth of a point between two rows is within what the valuation times alone produce. 70/30 with the first-ranked funds splits its equity fund's return the same way. Region weights are each fund's latest, held for the record so far and kept at each month end. The S&P 500, Nasdaq-100 and Mag7 funds count as North America, a LifeStrategy fund's equity part takes its underlying funds' regions, a FTSE All-World fund taking the held fund's and a FTSE Developed World fund the MSCI World fund's (method/lifestrategy_underlying.csv), and the SPDR funds' come from their holdings by country through method/msci_regions.csv.

For the global market portfolio, the difference runs through the two funds held at its equity-like share, equities, private equity and real estate together, which gives cash, split and the dollar by the same definitions. Equity classes is the weight of private equity and of real estate times each fund's return in euro minus the equity fund's, and bond classes is the weight of each bond class times its fund's return in euro minus the bond fund's. Each dollar fund's conversion to euro goes to the dollar, less the equity fund's for an equity-like class, so the currencies inside a bond fund's dollar return stay in bond classes. Regions and within each region are zero for it.

For savings, the bond fund's return against the rate is split three ways at the ECB curve's spot rate at the bond fund's duration in whole years: the rate against the bonds' yield earned over the days, the duration times the change in that yield, and the rest of the bond return. The LifeStrategy funds hold global bonds hedged to the euro, which the ECB curve does not price, so their bond rows are n/a and their bonds' difference to the bond fund stays within each region.

Timing of contributions is the comparator's money-weighted return minus its time-weighted return, less the portfolio's. Compounding is the difference of the cumulative returns minus the sum of the daily effects. An effect that does not apply to a comparator is zero. Effects are in percentage points to two decimals. What the portfolio does not do, and why

Added on 6 November 2026, after the first cycle day, in answer to the questions readers ask most. The rules are unchanged.

No optimiser. The split is set by the risk limit: 70 per cent equity is the largest target weight whose worst fall on the test's history stayed within 35 per cent (Figure 36). An optimiser would need the expected return of each sleeve, and that is the input the data cannot supply. The standard error of the equity series' mean yearly return over the test's 27 years is about 2.8 percentage points, computed as the series' yearly volatility over the square root of its years, against a gap in growth per year of 2.5 points between the 40/60 and the 100/0 split on the same history (the table under Figure 36). DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal measured how much history a sample-based optimiser needs before it beats an equal split out of sample: about 3,000 months for 25 assets and about 6,000 for 50.3 A replication of their study by the same author finds that none of the seven optimising rules it tests earns a higher Sharpe ratio than the equal split at the 5 per cent level, on any of four sets of assets, over the 261 months since the paper.4 The frontier of the two sleeves is in the Risk view (Figure 31). With two assets it is a line, and the limit picks the point on it.

The benchmark, and why not the S&P 500. A benchmark is the portfolio a client would hold without the manager, so that the manager's contribution can be measured as the difference and bounded. This portfolio's benchmark is the blend of its two funds' own indices at 70/30, rebalanced at each month end, the policy the rules implement. The difference to it is the cost of implementing that policy: the funds' costs and tracking differences, the band's drift from 70/30, cash waiting for the cycle day, and trading. The rules take no position against the benchmark, so there is no active return to claim, and the difference is expected to be small and negative. The two reference portfolios split it into its parts: reference A, the same contributions under the same rules at net asset value, and reference B, the same contributions returned to 70/30 on every cycle day (Figure 3). No single market index is the benchmark. The question readers ask most is why the portfolio does not simply hold the S&P 500. The global equity fund already holds the index's companies at about two thirds of its weight, so holding the index alone sells the other third of the world's listed companies and puts the whole portfolio into one country and one currency. A weight on one country above its share of the world is a view, and the rules take none. The advice to hold the index alone grew popular in the years after 2009, when the US stock market returned 597.7 per cent in dollars, dividends included, against 133.0 per cent for the developed markets outside the United States, and over the ten years from 2000 to 2009 the same two series returned minus 3.7 per cent and 29.6 per cent.5 On the limit, Figure 39 repeats the allocation test with the US stock market in euro in place of the global series, with the same splits, the same band rule and the same months: the largest split within 35 per cent is 65/35, and the worst fall at 70/30 is 36.0 per cent, from August 2000 to February 2003. Figure 40 shows what the same contributions on the same days would be in HSBC S&P 500 UCITS ETF USD (Acc), the S&P 500 ETF that the selection criteria of the rules pick, at its net asset value in euro and without costs, beside reference A. The two lines are two rule sets over the record's months, a span on which the gap says nothing about either.

No views. A view is a statement that an asset's expected return differs from what its price implies, held with a stated confidence. The standard method for turning views into weights starts from the market portfolio and tilts it by each view in proportion to the confidence in it.6 With no views the method returns the market portfolio, and that is what the equity sleeve holds, at the weights of the world's listed companies. The one judgement in the rules is the one behind holding equity at all, that its return exceeds the return on bonds over five to ten years, and the limit sets how much of the portfolio rides on it.

No signal. A signal is a view produced by a rule applied to data and tested on history. None enters the portfolio. The value of a forecasting skill grows with the square root of the number of independent bets it is applied to,7 and a portfolio of two funds with one decision a year makes about one bet a year, so no skill would be detectable in its record and none would be worth its cost. At this portfolio's size the orders a signal calls for cost more than the signal could earn, and the cost of each order is shown under Costs in the Performance view. A portfolio that carries a signal also becomes a test of that signal, and a few years of one portfolio cannot run that test. Signals belong in a separate study on decades of data, and a signal that passes its test there can enter the portfolio only through the yearly review of the rules, as a dated amendment with its test fixed in advance.8 The first review is in October 2027 and its agenda is public.

The rules

Rendered from rules/RULES.md at build time, with the amendments table

These are the rules the portfolio runs under. They were fixed on 7 October 2026, before the first order, and the git tag rules-v1.0 marks this version. A rule changes only through rule 9.

The portfolio is long-only and holds two exchange-traded funds in a euro brokerage account. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund whose shares are listed on a stock exchange; both ETFs held here are UCITS funds, the European Union standard for funds sold to the public. One ETF holds global equities and the other holds euro government bonds.

Terms used in the rules

  • Sleeve: the part of the portfolio held in one ETF. The portfolio has an equity sleeve and a bond sleeve.
  • Weight: a sleeve's value divided by the value of the portfolio. The value of the portfolio is the value of both ETFs plus any contribution not yet invested.
  • Target weight: 70 per cent for the equity sleeve and 30 per cent for the bond sleeve.
  • Band: the interval from 65 to 75 per cent for the equity weight.
  • Worst fall: the largest fall in the portfolio's value from a previous peak to a later low, measured on returns with contributions removed.
  • Contribution: money paid into the portfolio, meaning the starting amount and each top-up.
  • Top-up: the fixed monthly contribution.
  • Cycle day: the day each month on which the rules are applied.
  • Trading day: a day on which Xetra, the Frankfurt exchange where both ETFs are listed in euro, is open.
  • Order: an instruction to the broker to buy or sell one ETF.
  • Ledger: the private record of every contribution and every order.

Objective and risk

The mandate in mandate/MANDATE.md sets the objective and the risk limit. The objective is the highest long-run growth within a worst fall of about one third, over a horizon of five to ten years with no withdrawals, and a worst fall above 40 per cent is outside it. The target weights follow from the limit: 70 per cent is the largest equity target weight whose worst fall, in euro from February 1999 to December 2025 and rebalanced by a band as in rule 5, stayed within 35 per cent (34.7 per cent, from August 2000 to March 2003). The test and its data sources are in mandate/allocation_check.py, and its output is in mandate/allocation_check_results.md. The limit is the basis of the target weights and never a reason to sell. In a fall, rule 8 applies and the cycle runs as written.

The rules

  1. Target weights. The portfolio holds two sleeves, each in one ETF, at target weights of 70 per cent equity and 30 per cent bonds. There is no cash target. Cash is whatever part of a contribution has not yet been invested.

  2. Contributions. A starting amount is fixed before the first order. Every month after that, a top-up equal to 5 per cent of the starting amount is paid into the account by the 2nd. The euro amounts are private, and the fingerprint in the last section fixes them.

  3. The cycle. The cycle day is the 5th of each month, or the first trading day after the 5th when the 5th is not a trading day. On the cycle day the weights are computed from the ledger and the orders the rules produce are placed the same day.

  4. Top-ups rebalance first. The top-up buys the sleeve furthest below its target weight, up to the amount that brings that sleeve to its target weight. Any remainder buys both sleeves in proportion to their target weights. Nothing is sold while the equity weight is inside the band. With two sleeves, this places the portfolio at its target weights whenever the top-up is large enough, and otherwise moves it as close to them as buying alone allows. For example, with the starting amount set to 100, equity worth 68 and bonds worth 32 before a top-up of 5, the portfolio is worth 105 after the top-up and the equity target is 73.5. Equity is 5.5 short, more than the top-up, so all 5 buy equity and the equity weight moves from 68.0 to 69.5 per cent.

  5. The band. If the equity weight is below 65 or above 75 per cent after the top-up, the portfolio is brought back to exactly 70 and 30 per cent on the same day, by selling the sleeve above its target weight and buying the other. Inside the band nothing is sold. There is no calendar rebalancing. The band is the only trigger.

  6. Order budget. At most five orders are placed in a month, the number the account's plan carries without commission. If the rules call for more, the band orders come first and the top-up waits for the next cycle day. Rules 4 and 5 never need more than two orders on a cycle day.

  7. Execution. Orders are placed on a trading day between 15:45 and 17:00 Amsterdam time, when Xetra and the US equity market are both open, outside the first fifteen minutes of the US session and the last thirty minutes of the Xetra session. For each order the ledger records the date, the time, the ISIN, the side, the units, the price, the fee, the currency, and the bid and ask quoted when the order was placed.

  8. No discretion. No order is placed outside these rules. News does not add a purchase, a fall in prices does not pause the cycle, and no ETF is changed. When in doubt, the cycle is run as written and the doubt is recorded in the monthly log.

  9. Changing the rules. A rule changes only by a dated amendment committed to this file. An amendment applies from the first cycle day at least 30 days after its commit. The first review of the rules is on the cycle day of October 2027, twelve months after this version, and there is no review before it.

  10. Sales. Every sale is recorded in the ledger on its day with the units sold, the proceeds, the cost basis and the realised gain or loss. The cost basis is the average price paid per unit, fees included, times the units sold.

The TER (total expense ratio) is the yearly fee the fund deducts from its assets. An accumulating ETF reinvests the dividends or coupons it receives and pays nothing out. Physical replication means the fund holds the securities of its index: all of them under full replication, or a representative subset under sampling. The equity index covers large and mid-sized companies in developed and emerging markets. The bond index covers fixed-rate, investment-grade bonds of eurozone governments with more than one year to maturity. Fund size counts all share classes of the fund, as the issuer's factsheet reports it on the date shown.

How the two ETFs were chosen

Criteria. An equity ETF qualifies if it tracks a global index of developed and emerging markets (FTSE All-World, MSCI ACWI, MSCI ACWI IMI, or an equivalent global index tracked by a Vanguard, iShares or SPDR fund), is a UCITS fund, accumulates, is listed in euro on a European exchange, replicates physically, charges a TER of at most 0.25%, has a fund size above EUR 1 billion and is offered in euro on the account. A bond ETF qualifies if it tracks a euro aggregate or euro government index of investment-grade bonds, either across all maturities or capped at 10 years, is a UCITS fund, accumulates, holds bonds denominated in euro, charges a TER of at most 0.20%, has a fund size above EUR 500 million and is offered in euro on the account. Versions of an index that change its exposure fail: ESG and other screened indices, indices built on a factor (a company characteristic, such as dividend yield, by which an index selects or weights companies) and currency-hedged share classes. An index of short maturities only or long maturities only fails. Synthetic replication, in which the fund holds other securities and receives the index return from a bank through a swap contract, fails.

Ranking. Among the ETFs that qualify, the order was fixed before the funds were compared. Equity ETFs rank first by Irish domicile, then by the highest average tracking difference over 2023, 2024 and 2025, then by the lowest TER. Bond ETFs rank by the highest average tracking difference over the same years, then by the lowest TER. Tracking difference is an ETF's calendar-year return minus the return of its index, both taken from the issuer's factsheet, so a higher figure means a smaller shortfall. An ETF with fewer than three full calendar years of returns ranks after the others, by TER. Irish domicile comes first for equity because a fund domiciled in Ireland pays 15 per cent tax on US dividends under the treaty between the two countries, where a fund domiciled in Luxembourg pays 30 per cent. With the United States at about 60 per cent of a global index and a dividend yield near 1.3 per cent, the difference is about 0.12 percentage points a year.

Terms

The terms the dashboard uses, each with its definition.

Portfolio
the two ETFs held under these rules, plus any contribution not yet invested.
Sleeve
the part of the portfolio held in one ETF.
Weight
a sleeve's value divided by the value of the portfolio.
Target weight
the weight the rules aim for, 70 per cent equity and 30 per cent bonds.
Band
the interval from 65 to 75 per cent for the equity weight. When the equity weight is outside it on a cycle day, after the top-up, the portfolio is brought back to its target weights.
Rebalancing
moving the weights back towards the target weights, through top-ups (rule 4) or through the band (rule 5).
Contribution
money paid into the portfolio, meaning the starting amount and each top-up.
Starting amount
the first contribution, fixed before the first order.
Top-up
the fixed monthly contribution, 5 per cent of the starting amount.
Cycle day
the day each month on which the rules are applied.
Trading day
a day on which Xetra, the Frankfurt exchange where both ETFs are listed in euro, is open.
Ledger
the private record of every contribution and every order, kept outside the repository.
Record
what the program computes from the ledger and the prices: holdings, weights, orders and their costs, returns, the look-through and the reference portfolios.
Net asset value
the value of one share of an ETF at the close of a day, as its issuer publishes it.
Look-through
the portfolio's exposure to the securities inside the two ETFs, each ETF's holdings weighted by the ETF's weight in the portfolio.
Reference portfolio
a portfolio computed from the same contributions and prices as the portfolio, without costs. Reference portfolio A follows the same rules, and reference portfolio B goes back to the target weights on every cycle day.
S&P 500 alternative
the same contributions on the same days into one S&P 500 ETF at its net asset value in euro, in fractional units and without costs, starting with the starting amount at the net asset value of the day of the first purchase, exactly as reference portfolio A starts. It stands for the rule "hold the S&P 500 and nothing else" with no costs, so that it is compared with reference portfolio A like for like, two rule sets on the same money.
Index blend
70 per cent MSCI ACWI net total return and 30 per cent the Bloomberg Euro-Aggregate Treasury index, in euro, rebalanced at each month end, the portfolio's benchmark.
Implementation cost
reference portfolio A's value relative to the portfolio's, with contributions removed, in basis points, positive when the portfolio's value is below reference portfolio A's and negative when it is above.
Half-spread
half the gap between the ask and the bid quoted when an order is placed.
Basis point
one hundredth of a percentage point.
Split
the pair of target weights, written equity/bonds, for example 70/30.
Risk limit
the worst fall the mandate aims to stay within, about one third, tested as 35 per cent.
Growth per year
the compound annual return, with contributions removed.
Drawdown
the fall in the portfolio's value below its previous peak on a given day, with contributions removed, in per cent.
Worst fall
the largest drawdown, meaning the largest fall in the portfolio's value from a previous peak to a later low, with contributions removed.
Tracking difference
an ETF's return minus its index's return over a calendar year, in percentage points.
Comparator
a portfolio that receives the same contributions on the same days as the portfolio, valued in euro without costs and set beside the record in the Comparisons part, such as a reference portfolio, another split under the same rules, the global market portfolio, or savings at the euro short-term rate.
Global market portfolio
every asset class held at its share of the world's invested market value, here by the weights of Doeswijk, Lam and Swinkels (2014, data update 2024) at the end of 2023, held through one ETF per class.
Equity classes
for the global market portfolio, the weight of private equity and of real estate times each class fund's return in euro minus the held equity ETF's, less each fund's dollar conversion beyond the equity ETF's, which goes to the dollar.
Bond classes
for the global market portfolio, the weight of each bond class times its fund's return in euro minus the held bond ETF's, less each fund's conversion from US dollars, which goes to the dollar.
Euro short-term rate
the rate at which banks borrow euro overnight without collateral, which the European Central Bank publishes each working day.
Regions
the effect of holding the four MSCI regions in other weights than the held equity ETF does: the comparator's equity share times the sum over the regions of its weight minus the held ETF's, times the region's index return in euro, the allocation term of Brinson and Fachler (1985).
Within each region
what a comparator's difference to the two ETFs held at its equity share leaves after regions and the dollar: the choice of companies inside each region, the funds' costs and their timing against the indices.
Tracking error
the standard deviation of the daily differences between two series' returns, times the square root of 252, so a figure for a year.
Band chance alone produces
plus or minus two tracking errors times the square root of the years, around zero, the difference between two series that chance alone produces over the same span. It ignores compounding and treats the daily differences as independent.
Duration
a bond's average time to its payments, weighted by their value, in years. It is also, approximately, the percentage fall in the bond's price when its yield rises by one percentage point. For the bond sleeve it is the average duration in the issuer's factsheet.
Factor
one of the return series in Kenneth French's data library for developed markets: the market's return minus the risk-free rate, and the returns of long-short portfolios for size, value, profitability, investment and momentum.
Loading
the coefficient of the ETF's return minus the risk-free rate on a factor's return.
Risk-free rate
the one-month US Treasury bill return in French's files.
Standard error
the estimated standard deviation of an estimate.
Allocation test
the historical test in mandate/allocation_check.py that derived the split from the risk limit, on monthly euro returns from February 1999 to December 2025.
Bootstrap
the resampling of the allocation test's returns in blocks of twelve consecutive months, each starting at a random month, into paths of five and ten years, with the worst fall measured on each path.
Mechanics simulation
the rules applied to the allocation test's returns, with a starting amount of 100 and a top-up of 5 a month, for the counts of their orders.
Resampled paths
the bootstrap's ten-year paths carried forward from the first purchase with the top-up and the rules, in units of the starting amount.
In-sample
computed on the data on which a choice was made, here the returns from 1999 to 2025 on which the split was chosen.
Time-weighted return
the return with contributions removed, chained from day to day, so the timing and the size of the contributions do not affect it.
Market effect
a month's change in the portfolio's value that did not come from contributions, in per cent of the value at the start of the month.
Money-weighted return
the rate at which the contributions, each from its date, grow to the portfolio's value, so the timing of the contributions counts. Cumulative in the first year and per year after it.
Cycle report
the file cycles/YYYY-MM.md written on a cycle day before any order, with each rule that bears on the proposed orders, its figure and a pass or a fail, and one line after the orders that compares them with the proposal.
Monthly note
the note of 120 to 200 words, in notes/YYYY-MM.md, written by hand after each cycle day.
Departure
an order placed, or left unplaced, other than as the rules require. The departures register lists each one with its rule and its consequence.
Rolling correlation
the correlation of two series of monthly returns over the 36 months to each month end.
Component
one of the parts into which the look-through divides the portfolio to estimate its risk: the four equity regions, the bond sleeve's three maturity groups, and cash, which has no return.
Maturity group
the bonds of the bond sleeve with 1 to 5, 5 to 10 or over 10 years to maturity, each priced as a zero-coupon euro area government bond at 3, 7.5 or 20 years from the European Central Bank's yield curve.
Estimated volatility
the standard deviation of the portfolio's return over a year, estimated from the components' monthly returns over the last 120 months at the latest look-through weights.
Realised volatility
the standard deviation of the portfolio's last 12 monthly time-weighted returns, times the square root of 12, so a figure for a year.
Value at risk
the loss over one month that the portfolio, at today's weights, exceeded in one month in twenty of the 120-month window, read from those months or from the normal distribution with the estimated volatility.
Expected shortfall
the mean loss over one month in the months beyond the value at risk, from the window or from the normal distribution.
Forecast's calibration
the standard deviation, over the months that have a forecast, of each month's return divided by the monthly volatility estimated for it at the end of the month before. 1 means the estimates matched the returns, and above 1 that they were too low.
Covariance
how far two returns move together, in the units of variance, the square of volatility: their correlation times the product of their standard deviations.
Risk contribution
a component's weight times the covariance of its return with the portfolio's return, divided by the portfolio's variance. The contributions sum to 100 per cent.
Efficient frontier
the long-only, fully invested portfolios of the components with the lowest estimated volatility for each estimated return, on the means and covariances of one window of 120 months.
Minimum-variance portfolio
the portfolio on the efficient frontier with the lowest estimated volatility.
Sharpe ratio
the mean of a portfolio's return above the risk-free rate, divided by the standard deviation of that excess return, so the return earned per unit of volatility.
Cap-weighted
holding each security in proportion to its market value, as the indices of both ETFs do.
TER
total expense ratio, the management and other operating costs an ETF takes from its assets each year, as a percentage of them.
Ongoing costs
what an ETF takes from its assets each year, its TER plus its transaction costs, as its key information document states them. They are inside its net asset value.
Drift effect
reference portfolio A's return minus reference portfolio B's, the effect of letting the weights drift inside the band, before costs.
